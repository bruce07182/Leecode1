Chapter 21 · Electric charge and Coulomb’s law

Essential ideas first. Open any concept for definitions, reasoning, examples, and exam traps.

Charge is quantized and conserved

Charge comes in integer multiples of the elementary charge. Total charge of an isolated system stays constant.

q = ne; e = 1.602 × 10⁻¹⁹ C
Understand this concept · definitions and why

n is an integer; e is the magnitude of the electron charge. Electrons have −e and protons +e.

Worked example / quick check

If an object gains 3 electrons, its charge changes by −3e ≈ −4.81 × 10⁻¹⁹ C.

Common mistake / exam hint

Quantized does not mean every macroscopic charge is small; an object contains huge numbers of charges.

Coulomb’s law

Point charges attract if their signs differ and repel if their signs match.

F = k |q₁q₂| / r²
Understand this concept · definitions and why

k = 1/(4πε₀) ≈ 8.99 × 10⁹ N·m²/C²; r is separation. The force acts along the line joining the charges.

Worked example / quick check

If r doubles, force becomes F/4; if one charge doubles too, the result is F/2.

Common mistake / exam hint

For multiple charges, calculate each force as a vector and add components.

Chapter 22 · Electric fields

Essential ideas first. Open any concept for definitions, reasoning, examples, and exam traps.

Definition of electric field

The electric field is force per unit positive test charge; units N/C.

E⃗ = F⃗ / q₀
Understand this concept · definitions and why

q₀ is a small positive test charge. A negative charge feels force opposite E⃗.

Worked example / quick check

A +2 μC charge in a 300 N/C field feels F = 6 × 10⁻⁴ N along the field.

Common mistake / exam hint

The source creates E; the test charge experiences F = qE.

Field of a point charge

Field points outward from +Q and inward toward −Q.

E = k |Q| / r²
Understand this concept · definitions and why

Q is the source charge; r is distance from it. This magnitude formula assumes a point charge or spherical exterior.

Worked example / quick check

At 2r, E becomes one-fourth as strong.

Common mistake / exam hint

Electric field is a vector: add x and y components when several charges contribute.

Superposition

Find the field from each source independently and add the vectors.

E⃗net = Σ E⃗ᵢ
Understand this concept · definitions and why

For continuous charge, replace the sum with ∫ dE⃗. Symmetry often cancels components.

Worked example / quick check

At the midpoint between two identical positive charges, the net E is zero.

Common mistake / exam hint

Zero field does not imply zero electric potential.

Continuous charge distributions

Break a charged line, surface, or volume into tiny charge elements and integrate.

dq = λ dl = σ dA = ρ dV
Understand this concept · definitions and why

λ is charge per length (C/m), σ per area (C/m²), and ρ per volume (C/m³). Each tiny element creates dE = k dq/r² in its radial direction.

Worked example / quick check

For a symmetric charged ring, transverse field components cancel on the axis.

Common mistake / exam hint

Keep track of distance and angle for each dq; they may vary across the object.

Electric dipole

An electric dipole is two equal opposite charges separated by distance d.

p = qd; τ = pE sinθ
Understand this concept · definitions and why

The dipole moment vector points from − to +. In a uniform field it feels torque τ⃗ = p⃗ × E⃗.

Worked example / quick check

Torque is zero when the dipole aligns with the field.

Common mistake / exam hint

Net force is zero in a uniform field, but torque can be nonzero.

Chapter 23 · Gauss’s law and its applications

Essential ideas first. Open any concept for definitions, reasoning, examples, and exam traps.

Electric flux

Flux measures the electric field passing through a surface.

ΦE = ∫ E⃗ · dA⃗
Understand this concept · definitions and why

dA⃗ points normal to the surface; for a uniform field through a flat area, ΦE = EA cosθ.

Worked example / quick check

A field parallel to a flat surface produces zero flux through it.

Common mistake / exam hint

θ is measured from the surface normal, not the surface itself.

Gauss’s law

Net flux through any closed surface depends only on net charge enclosed.

∮ E⃗ · dA⃗ = Qenc / ε₀
Understand this concept · definitions and why

∮ means integrate over a closed surface; Qenc is algebraic net charge inside; ε₀ ≈ 8.85 × 10⁻¹² C²/(N·m²).

Worked example / quick check

Enclosing +2 nC gives ΦE ≈ 226 N·m²/C.

Common mistake / exam hint

Gauss’s law is always true, but solving for E directly needs enough symmetry.

Isolated conductor

In electrostatic equilibrium the field inside conducting material is zero; excess charge resides on its surface.

Einside = 0; Eoutside = σ / ε₀
Understand this concept · definitions and why

Just outside a conductor, the electric field is perpendicular to the surface. The entire conductor is an equipotential.

Worked example / quick check

Inside the metal of a charged hollow conducting sphere, E = 0.

Common mistake / exam hint

A cavity containing a charge can have a nonzero field in the empty cavity; distinguish cavity from metal.

Infinite line of charge

The field of a long straight uniform line decreases as 1/r.

E = |λ| / (2πε₀r)
Understand this concept · definitions and why

λ is linear charge density; use a coaxial cylindrical Gaussian surface. Field points radially outward for positive λ.

Worked example / quick check

Doubling r halves E.

Common mistake / exam hint

This is an ideal infinite line (or an approximation far from the ends).

Infinite charged sheet

A uniformly charged infinite nonconducting sheet creates a distance-independent field on either side.

E = |σ| / (2ε₀)
Understand this concept · definitions and why

Use a pillbox Gaussian surface. For a conductor surface, the field just outside is |σ|/ε₀ instead.

Worked example / quick check

At twice the distance from an ideal infinite sheet, E is unchanged.

Common mistake / exam hint

Do not mix up an insulating sheet with the surface of a conductor.

Spherical shell

A uniformly charged thin spherical shell acts like a point charge outside and has zero field everywhere inside.

E = k|Q|/r² outside; E = 0 inside
Understand this concept · definitions and why

r is measured from the center; outside means r > R, inside means r < R. Symmetry is essential.

Worked example / quick check

At r = 2R, E = k|Q|/(4R²).

Common mistake / exam hint

For a conducting spherical shell, the same zero-interior result holds when there is no charge inside its cavity.

Uniformly charged solid sphere

For a nonconducting solid sphere with uniform volume charge, field grows linearly with r inside.

E = k|Q|r/R³ inside; E = k|Q|/r² outside
Understand this concept · definitions and why

R is sphere radius, r is distance from center. Qenc = Q(r³/R³) for r < R.

Worked example / quick check

At r = R/2, the inside field is half the surface field.

Common mistake / exam hint

A conducting solid sphere is different: E = 0 inside the metal.

Chapter 24 · Electric potential

Essential ideas first. Open any concept for definitions, reasoning, examples, and exam traps.

Potential and voltage

Potential is energy per unit charge; voltage is a difference in potential.

V = U/q; ΔV = ΔU/q
Understand this concept · definitions and why

V is measured in volts (J/C), U in joules, q in coulombs. The zero of potential is a reference choice.

Worked example / quick check

A +2 C charge moving through ΔV = −3 V has ΔU = −6 J.

Common mistake / exam hint

Potential is a scalar, unlike the vector electric field.

Potential of point charges

Potential contributions add as signed numbers.

V = kQ/r; Vtotal = Σ kQᵢ/rᵢ
Understand this concept · definitions and why

For a positive point charge V is positive relative to zero at infinity; for a negative charge it is negative.

Worked example / quick check

Contributions +4 V and −6 V yield −2 V.

Common mistake / exam hint

Do not use absolute value on Q when computing potential.

Field from potential

The electric field points in the direction of steepest decrease of potential.

Eₓ = −dV/dx
Understand this concept · definitions and why

In 3D, E⃗ = −∇V. Equipotential surfaces are perpendicular to the field.

Worked example / quick check

If potential falls 20 V over +4 m in a uniform field, Eₓ = +5 V/m.

Common mistake / exam hint

A negative charge accelerates opposite the field direction.

Potential of a continuous distribution

Add tiny scalar potential contributions from each charge element.

V = ∫ k dq/r
Understand this concept · definitions and why

Choose dq = λ dl, σ dA, or ρ dV. Integrating potential is often simpler than integrating field vectors.

Worked example / quick check

Equal +Q and −Q at equal distance contribute potentials that cancel.

Common mistake / exam hint

The r in the integrand is distance from each charge element to the observation point.

Potential energy and conductors

Changes in potential energy determine electric work; an electrostatic conductor is equipotential.

ΔU = qΔV; Vconductor = constant
Understand this concept · definitions and why

Work done by the electric field is Wfield = −ΔU. The electric field inside the conductor is zero.

Worked example / quick check

For q = −2 C and ΔV = +3 V, ΔU = −6 J.

Common mistake / exam hint

A negative charge loses potential energy when moving to higher electric potential.
